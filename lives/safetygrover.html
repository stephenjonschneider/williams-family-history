GRAVESEND & MIDDLETOWN · c.1658 – AFTER 1742

Captain Safety Grover

Militia officer and magistrate, and one of the men who took the Governor prisoner

Safety Grover was born at Gravesend about 1658; in April 1707 he swore in court that he was 49. He was the youngest son of James Grover, and his unusual first name would be carried down for generations by the Bownes, Bordens, Stouts and Laytons.

He came to Middletown as a boy. In 1680 the governor ordered land laid out for “Safety Grover the sonn of James,” a grant that counted a wife, so he was married by then. By 1683 he was sitting on county juries, and on 3 December 1683, under New Jersey's first militia act, he was commissioned ensign of the Middletown company, with his father as lieutenant and Captain John Bowne as major.

His first appearance in court as a defendant is a good story. In 1685 Samuel Leonard accused him of “ranging the woods”: catching and branding a wild horse. His father, sitting as a justice, certified that Safety had brought the horse before him and been allowed to brand it. The jury twice returned the same verdict of guilty, and in June 1685 he was fined five pounds.

That November his father gave him land by a deed to “my son Safety Grover,” and in 1686 the Proprietors patented him tracts along the Mill Brook, on the Navesink and at Shoal Harbour. He was called Captain by 1688, was commissioned lieutenant of Capt. Samuel Leonard's company in 1692, and by 1695 “Capt. Safety Grover” was guardian of an orphan boy and one of the nearest relations of his late brother Joseph's children.

The defining moment of his life came in 1701. Middletown's settlers held their land under the old Nicolls patent and refused the Proprietors' quitrents and courts. In 1700, when Governor Andrew Hamilton marched on Middletown with thirty or forty armed men, he found nearly two hundred townsmen waiting with clubs and withdrew. On 25 March 1701 Hamilton returned to sit in the Middletown court with Lewis Morris, Samuel Leonard and the justices. They were trying Moses Butterworth, who had confessed to sailing with Captain William Kidd. An innkeeper, Samuel Willett, declared that the Governor had no authority to hold court. A drummer struck up, thirty or forty men came up the stairs, and in a scuffle with drawn swords Benjamin and Richard Borden carried off the prisoner, both of them wounded. The court clerk then recorded that “Safety Grover” and the others, “to the number of about one hundred persons, did traitorously seize the Governor and the Justices,” the King's Attorney General, the clerk and the under-sheriff, and kept them under guard until 29 March.

Safety's name heads that list. With him were his Borden kin, Obadiah Bowne, Jonathan and James Stout, and his nephew James Grover. The uprising helped end proprietary rule: in 1702 the Proprietors surrendered the government of New Jersey to the Crown.

The rebel became a pillar of the county. By 1711 he was “Safety Grover of Middletown, gentleman,” buying up the Shoal Harbour meadows beside his father's old land. In March 1714/15 he signed among the justices who ordered the first court house and gaol for Monmouth. In 1720 he bought a share of a proprietary right, the very kind of title his neighbours had once resisted, and in the 1720s he served as juryman, overseer of the highways and bondsman. In 1722 his brother-in-law William Winter released to him his half of ten acres they had taken up together. He lived to a great age: in 1742, at about 84, he and his son James still owed a bond to the estate of William Covenhoven.

His wife was probably named Mercy. Their daughter Hannah married William Taylor, and their son Safety Taylor, who died in 1766, carried his grandfather's name. Another daughter, whose first name no record gives, married her cousin Safety Borden of Bordentown, and in 1747 their daughter Amy's husband, Daniel Herbert, wrote in his will of the goods given to Amy by “her honoured grandfather Safety Grover.” A third daughter, Esther, whom the documents support as Safety's daughter, married Samuel Bowne and named a son Safety Bowne. Through that line the name, and Safety's blood, came into the Laytons. A Mordecai Grover, a witness at Middletown in 1705, was probably a son as well.

The name outlived him by two centuries. Amy's son Safety Meghee, a Bordentown cordwainer and tanner, named his own son Safety in his will of 1770, and the name went with the family to Pennsylvania, Ohio and Illinois; Safety McGee Henderson of White Hall, Illinois, lived until 1908. Twenty-four men named Safety have now been traced, and every one whose line is known descends from James Grover.
