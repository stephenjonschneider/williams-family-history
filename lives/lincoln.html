FREEHOLD TO SPRINGFIELD · 1716 – 1865

Abraham Lincoln

The President whose father’s line began in Monmouth County, and how he is a cousin of this family

Abraham Lincoln never knew it, but his father's family had started out in Monmouth County. His great-grandfather John Lincoln, the man later called “Virginia John,” was born at Freehold on 3 May 1716, and through his mother he was a great-grandson of Captain John Bowne, one of the men who bought Monmouth.

The link runs through two Monmouth women. Captain John Bowne's daughter Sarah was born at Gravesend on 27 November 1669 and came to Middletown as a baby. She married Richard Salter, a lawyer, judge and member of the colonial Assembly, and their daughter Hannah married Mordecai Lincoln, a young ironworker from Hingham, Massachusetts, who had come to Monmouth with his brother Abraham. They were married by September 1714, when Hannah's uncle John Bowne, the merchant of Matawan, made his will and left a legacy to “Hannah Lincon.” The same will's inventory lists his debtors, a roll call of the county that includes James Grover, Stouts, Bordens, Laytons and Williamses.

The Lincolns stayed in New Jersey about seven years. A little daughter died there and was buried in Monmouth. About 1720 the family moved west to the Schuylkill country of Pennsylvania, to what became Coventry Township in Chester County. Hannah died about 1727, and Mordecai, who married again, died at Amity in Berks County in 1736.

Their only son, John, grew up in Pennsylvania and in 1743 married Rebecca (Flowers) Morris, a young widow. In 1765 he sold the last of his Pennsylvania land, and in 1768 he bought six hundred acres on the west side of Linville Creek, a few miles north of Harrisonburg in the Shenandoah Valley, in what became Rockingham County, Virginia. That is how he came to be called Virginia John.

His eldest son, Abraham, born in Berks County in 1744, served as a militia captain in the Revolution and about 1782 took his own family over the mountains to Kentucky. In May 1786, while he and his sons Mordecai, Josiah and Thomas were planting corn on their new land at Long Run, he was shot from the woods. Mordecai ran to the cabin, took up a rifle and shot the attacker as he reached for eight-year-old Thomas. The President later wrote that his grandfather was killed “not in battle but by stealth,” and that this story was the one most deeply fixed in his memory.

The widowed Bathsheba moved her children to Washington County, Kentucky, where the country was more settled. Thomas grew up to be a carpenter and farmer, married Nancy Hanks on 12 June 1806, and on 12 February 1809 their son Abraham was born in a log cabin in Hardin County. In 1816 Thomas moved the family across the Ohio to Little Pigeon Creek in southern Indiana. Nancy died there of milk sickness in 1818, and the next year Thomas married Sarah Bush Johnston, a widow with three children of her own.

The last move west came in the spring of 1830. On 1 March, with Abraham just turned twenty-one, the whole family, together with the families of his stepmother's two daughters and their husbands, left the Indiana homestead in wagons pulled by ox teams, and Abraham drove one of them. In March they reached Macon County, Illinois, and built a log cabin on the north side of the Sangamon River, about ten miles west of Decatur. After a hard year of fever and the bitter Winter of the Deep Snow, Thomas and Sarah moved on to Coles County, where they lived the rest of their lives. Abraham struck out on his own, settling at New Salem in July 1831 and moving to Springfield as a new lawyer in April 1837. The boy whose family had left Freehold three generations before was President of the United States by 1861.

He was curious about his roots. In 1848, as a congressman, he answered a letter from Solomon Lincoln of Hingham with all he knew: his grandfather had gone from Rockingham County, Virginia, to Kentucky about 1782. He then wrote to a Virginia cousin, David Lincoln, asking whether the family had come from Berks County, Pennsylvania, and whether they had been Quakers. He never learned that the line ran back through Freehold, Hannah Salter and the Bownes of Middletown.

Abraham married Mary Todd at Springfield on 4 November 1842. Of their four sons, Edward, William and Tad died young, and only Robert lived to have children. Robert's three children had three children between them, and none of those had children of their own. The President's line ended when his great-grandson Robert Todd Lincoln Beckwith died on Christmas Eve 1985.

How he is a cousin of this family. William Bowne and his wife Ann, who came from Salem to Gravesend, had sons John and James. James and his wife Mary Stout were the parents of Samuel Bowne, whose daughter Catherine married John Layton and carried the Bowne blood down to the Laytons, the Williamses and everyone who uses this app. John's daughter Sarah was Samuel's first cousin, so Hannah Salter Lincoln and Catherine Layton were second cousins, and Virginia John and the sons of John and Catherine Layton were third cousins, born in Monmouth a generation apart. Counting from the compiler, President Lincoln is a sixth cousin five times removed; for each generation younger, add one more “removed.” He shares no Stout or Grover blood with the family, only the Bownes.
