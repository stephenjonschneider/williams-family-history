SHREWSBURY & RUMSON NECK · BY 1668 – 1710/11

John Hance

A Rhode Island patentee’s share, a farm right across Rumson Neck, a place in the Manasquan Beach Company of 1685, and twenty years on the Monmouth bench

Where John Hance came from is still an open question. The family tree has him born at Dover, New Hampshire, in December 1643, but no record found so far says so. What the records do show points to New England. His first Monmouth land came from a Rhode Island man, and he looked after the affairs of Tobias Hanson of Piscataqua, the river Dover stands on, whom he named in his will. The tree calls his wife Elizabeth Hanson, which would explain that trust, but her surname still waits for proof.

On 7 August 1668, only three years after the Monmouth Patent was granted, John Hance was already “of Shrewsbury” when John Coggeshall of Rhode Island, one of the twelve patentees, sold him his whole share of land at Navesink, Rumson, Portapeck and the Gift Land. The next year Edward Wharton, a Salem Quaker, gave him an order to hold and enjoy his lot, and London heirs of the Whartons later made him their attorney. By 1677 the land lists show “John Hance, wife and man servant” with 360 acres. That April the Proprietors issued him warrants for 480 acres in the right of two other men, John Foxall and Turlough Swiny, where most of his neighbours claimed in right of themselves and their wives.

Those rights became his home. In November 1679 the surveyor laid out for him a strip about 145 chains long, close to two miles, and a quarter of a mile wide, running right across Rumson Neck from the Shrewsbury River to the Navesink, with Eliakim Wardell on the west and Hugh Dickman on the east and a highway passing through it. He took up a second tract of 156 acres on the Navesink side and seven acres in the Great Meadow. When the Proprietors had the strip re-surveyed in 1688 they called it the land “on which he liveth,” with a share of a little island called the False Beach in Shrewsbury Bay, and in 1695 he widened it with a 75-acre wedge on the west, “adjoining to the land where he lives.” His meadow also bounded the meadow of Samuel Wolcott, so the Hances and the Wolcotts were neighbours more than a century before their families joined.

He was one of the men who held the young county together. From May 1676 until about 1698 he signed some sixty Monmouth instruments as a justice, usually with John Throckmorton and Peter Tilton and later with Andrew Bowne: Indian deeds from the sachems of Wickatong and Ramesing, the 1679 division of the Middletown Indian purchases, a wedding in 1694 and a sloop master’s protest in 1697. Absent owners trusted him with their business. He sold a Portapeck plantation for the Wharton heirs in 1691, took a power of attorney from Samuel Webb in 1697, sold 200 acres by the sea for Tobias Hanson in 1699, and in 1701 was an executor of Abigail Lippincott.

His land reached well down the shore. He was one of the Navesink purchasers named in the warrant of 9 July 1685 for the Indian purchase at Manasquan, and every survey of his neighbours there, from 1685 to 1694, bounds on him. On 4 November 1687 the Proprietors patented to “John Hance of Shrewsbury … yeoman” his 250 acres in five tracts on the Manasquan River, among the lots of the Allens, Tobias Hansen, Edmond Lafetra, William Lawrence and Joseph West. He never lived there; it was an out-tract, still his in 1701. By 1690 he also held beach land at Barnegat and land at the head of Barnegat Bay by the Metedeconk. In December 1706, an old man, he bought a tenth of one twenty-fourth share of East Jersey, a right to take up more land in the Proprietors’ future dividends. On 12 March 1695/6 his daughter Elizabeth, written “Elizabeth Hance jr” to tell her from her mother, married John Worthley at the Friends’ meeting house in Shrewsbury. Her father and mother, her sister Judeth and her brother John all signed the certificate. Six months later the new son-in-law took a nine-year-old apprentice before the county court, with John Hance sitting as one of the justices.

John Hance made his will on 24 March 1707/8, “of Shrewsbury, yeoman.” He left his land in Shrewsbury and named his wife Elizabeth, his children John, Isaac, Mary Antrom, Elizabeth Worthly, Deborah Corleis and Judeth Hardman, and his old friend Tobias Hanson. Elizabeth and Isaac were his executors. The will was proved on 27 January 1710/11, and his personal estate came to £168 15s, nearly £48 of it in silver money, bills and bonds. His sons kept the family on the land: in 1718 a new highway ran past John Hance’s house on the North River, near the road from the Quaker meeting house to Red Bank, and Isaac held land on the road from Rumson to the Falls.

Elizabeth’s son Richard Worthley was the father of Lydia Worthley, and her daughter Lydia Borden married Amos Wolcott. That makes John Hance the compiler’s 9th great-grandfather. A 1904 article, “John Hance and Some of His Descendants,” first set out the family. The link from John to his daughter Elizabeth no longer rests on it alone: his will, the meeting’s own marriage register and the signed certificate prove it three times over.
