LYNN, GRAVESEND & MIDDLETOWN · c.1620s – 1685/6

James Grover

Rebel at Gravesend, founder of Middletown, tavern keeper, ironmaster and justice

Nobody knows where in England James Grover was born. He may be the James Grover, servant of James Hubbard, who in December 1642 bound himself to a carpenter at Lynn, Massachusetts, to learn the trade. Hubbard was at his side for the next twenty years.

By February 1646 he had a house lot and a twenty-acre farm in the first division of Gravesend, the English town Lady Deborah Moody had planted on Dutch Long Island. He was not a quiet neighbour. On 9 March 1655 he, Hubbard and George Baxter raised the English flag at Gravesend and proclaimed the English Republic. The others were arrested for treason; Grover escaped to Boston and on to England. In 1657 he came back with a letter from Oliver Cromwell to the English of Long Island. Governor Stuyvesant ordered him brought to New Amsterdam, but Grover slipped away and left the letter behind, and Stuyvesant sent it to Holland unopened.

When the English took New Netherland in 1664, Grover was among the men licensed to bargain with the Navesink sachems, and on 8 April 1665 he was one of the twelve patentees of the Monmouth purchase. When the accounts were settled in 1670, every purchaser's payment went to “James Grover & Company”: he, John Tilton and William Goulding had laid out the wampum, coats, kettles, guns and powder that paid the sachems.

He sold his Gravesend farm in 1667 and became one of the first settlers of Middletown. He helped survey the town lots, drew lot No. 16 and served as the first town clerk. On 10 July 1667 Governor Philip Carteret licensed “James Grouer of Nauesinc to keep an ordinary, or victualing house,” one of the earliest tavern licences on record in New Jersey. In 1669 he took on the town mill, promising to grind the townspeople's corn first and to make meal as good as any in New York. He was also one of the founders of the Baptist church at Middletown.

He could be on both sides of a quarrel. In 1668 he and John Bowne went to the first Assembly as Middletown's deputies and took the oath to the Proprietors. The town meeting disowned them, and the repudiation in the town book is signed “Testis James Grover, Towne Clarke.” That August he carried the settlers' letter about their land titles to Governor Nicolls.

On 24 August 1674 the sachems of the Toponemese sold Grover, John Bowne and Richard Hartshorne the land at “the Falls.” There, by tradition, Grover started the first ironworks in New Jersey, bringing the Leonard brothers from Massachusetts to work the bog iron. The venture needed more money than he had, and by 1675 the works had passed to Lewis Morris, whose Tinton Manor was built on them with the labour of enslaved Africans. The records held here do not yet show the transfer itself.

He rose with the colony. He was lieutenant of the Middletown foot company from 1672, built a mill on Staten Island for New York's governor in 1673, and that summer was chosen with John Bowne to treat with the Dutch when they briefly retook New York. From 1675 he sat as a justice of the county court, “Mr James Grover” beside Capt. John Bowne, and he patented more than a thousand acres, including his mill seat on the Navesink. In 1683 he was again lieutenant, with his son Safety as ensign.

On 18 November 1685 he gave land to “my son Safety Grover,” and on 1 December he made his will. He was buried before 28 December, when his executors paid for the funeral and for fencing the grave. His widow Rebecca was maintained from the estate, his sons rebuilt the house and the mill, and Lewis Morris sued the executors. He left five children: James, Joseph, Abigail (Mrs Benjamin Borden), Safety and Hannah.
