GRAVESEND & MIDDLETOWN · c.1620s – AFTER 1705

Penelope Stout

The “Mother of Middletown,” and the legend that grew around her

Every Stout descendant knows the story. A ship bound for New Amsterdam runs aground near Sandy Hook. The passengers walk on toward the Dutch town, but a young woman stays behind with her sick husband. Lenape men find them, kill him and leave her for dead. She hides for days in a hollow tree until an old Indian carries her to his village, heals her wounds and later brings her to New Amsterdam. There she meets and marries an Englishman, Richard Stout. Years afterward, when the family has settled across the bay, the same old man slips into her house to warn her of an attack, and the settlers are saved.

It is a gripping tale, but it was first printed in 1765 by the historian Samuel Smith, more than a century after the events it describes. In 1792 Morgan Edwards published a second version from Oliver Hart, the Hopewell minister, which gave her the surname “Van Princis” and the famous claim that she lived to 110. Later writers added a name for the first husband, a scalping and noble Dutch parents. The versions disagree on where the ship came ashore, how long she hid and who rescued her, and no record has been found of the wreck.

The records tell a quieter story. On 12 September 1648 the town court at Gravesend, on Dutch Long Island, questioned “Pennellope Prince” for saying that a neighbour's wife had milked Thomas Applegate's cow. She “acknowledged her fault” and made amends, and both sides were satisfied. Her name was Prince, an English name, which casts doubt on the Dutch heritage of the legend. She married Richard Stout, a Nottinghamshire man who had served seven years at sea and held a Gravesend plantation by 1643. Their children were born from about 1645 to 1669.

In 1665 Richard was one of the twelve patentees of the Monmouth purchase, and the Stouts were remembered as one of the first five families to settle on it. That December their daughter Mary married James Bowne at Gravesend, the first marriage joining two of this history's founding families.

At Middletown, Penelope appears in the records beside her husband. In 1679/80 she and Richard sold their house, barn and orchard. On 23 September 1684 she came before the justices herself to acknowledge the sale and the £66 5s paid. In 1690 Richard set aside a lot and meadow for her “joynture,” her support as a widow.

Richard's will of 9 June 1703, signed with his mark, left “my loving wife” the orchard, her rooms in the house, the cellar and the use of all his land for life. The will was proved in October 1705. Penelope was alive when he wrote it, and after that the records fall silent. Tradition places her grave at Middletown.

Whatever happened on the beach, the woman in the records is remarkable enough. She stood up in a Dutch town court, crossed the bay with the first settlers, raised a large family and outlived her husband. Her children's families spread through Monmouth and far beyond, and she is still remembered as the Mother of Middletown.
